  1.  Product Brief

What is it?

Pouch is a baby monitor that tracks an infant's growth and development from 1-24 months and identifies early signs of cerebral palsy (CP) and seizures. It consists of a camera mounted to a bassinet or crib and a companion app.

What will it do?

The monitor will be installed on a crib and will track the infant’s movements. Pouch then will flag early signs of cerebral palsy and autism so that infants can get the diagnosis immediately confirmed by a physician. It will also track the infant’s growth and provide sleep and temperature tracking.

How does it work?

Early markers of cerebral palsy in babies are called cramped synchronized general movements. These are leg and arm spasms an infant with CP has while lying down that most parents would interpret as normal movements, however they indicate cerebral palsy to a trained eye. Our model will be trained on online videos of these spasms, as well on videos of normal infant movement to determine whether or not the infant shows these markers.

Who is it for?

Pouch will be used by parents of infants and will be easily installable by anyone.

What makes it different?

This would be the first device that automatically screens for cerebral palsy and autism. It will offer many of the same features as other baby monitors, but has these added features.

Must have features

The device must be able to flag for early signs of cerebral palsy and seizures with 90% specificity and sensitivity.

What have we built?

We have developed an LSTM model that detects signs of cerebral palsy with 71% specificity and sensitivity. This is a proof of concept that shows that given more data, we can reach our 90% target.

2. Requirements specification

2.1 Context

Cerebral palsy is a condition that affects a person’s ability to move and balance. It is the most common motor disability of childhood[1] and affects approximately 1 in 345 Canadians. It is a disease infants acquire at birth. Infants' brain plasticity decreases significantly from 1 to 2 years old, so the earlier you can diagnose cerebral palsy and access early intervention physiotherapy the better the infant fares over their life.

Symptoms of cerebral palsy appear in infants as early as 9 weeks old[2]. These show up in the form of specific movements an infant makes in their crib called cramped synchronized and absent fidgety movements. These movements are non-obvious, and there is a standardized test called the General Movement Assessment that is used by specially trained doctors to identify these signs.

2.2 Problem statement

Signs of cerebral palsy in infants are often undiagnosed, as these characteristic movements are not obvious to parents and few doctors are trained in the General Movement Assessment. Infants are then only brought to the doctor later in their life, when parents realize that their child is not meeting important developmental milestones such as walking and sitting. As a consequence, the average age of diagnosis for cerebral palsy is 18 months[3]. Diagnosis at this age compared to diagnosis and treatment when symptoms first start showing up at 9 weeks can make the difference between a person with cerebral palsy being able to walk versus them needing to use a wheelchair or walker for the rest of their life.

Infants with epilepsy and autism also exhibit different movement-based symptoms[4] that largely go undiagnosed, leading to further complications for the rest of their lives.

2.3 Our Solution

There is thus a need for a more widely available diagnostic tool to detect early signs of cerebral palsy and other developmental diseases. We are developing the first baby monitor that detects signs of cerebral palsy, and hope to eventually expand to autism and infantile seizure detection. Our baby monitor will have all the functional aspects of a regular baby monitor such as recording baby video, sleep monitoring and cry detection, while detecting cramped synchronized movements and absent fidgety movements. We are doing this by using labeled videos of healthy infants and of infants with cerebral palsy to train a machine learning model to classify these movements.

We have developed a model on a limited dataset that can classify cerebral palsy symptoms vs. normal movements with 71% specificity and sensitivity. Our goal is to develop a larger dataset to be able to detect between these movements with upwards of 90% specificity and sensitivity.

2.4 Requirements

2.4.1 Functional requirements for MVP

Predicting fidgety movements vs cramped synchronized movements using video data

We are applying the General Movement Assessment, which looks at two main kinds of movements in infants: fidgety movements and cramped synchronized movements. If we cannot distinguish between both we will not be applying this medical test correctly.

Using a model that has been tried and tested in the real world

We want to use an artificial intelligence model that has already proven itself to be robust in real life applications since we are making a medical device.

Making predictions live faster than 5 frames per second using camera input feed when running on a laptop with intel i7 CPU

Our hardware available for verifying our requirements at the moment is computers with intel i7 CPUs and a Jetson Nano. If we cannot run live detection faster than 5 frames per second using a top CPU, we probably will not be able to run it on a slower CPU inside a baby monitor.

Specificity and sensitivity > 70%

We eventually want to be able to detect the difference between fidgety movements and cramped synchronized movements better than the average trained doctor. The average trained doctor completing the assessment has a specificity of 85%-95%. Given our lack of available data, we aim for a specificity and sensitivity >70% as a proof of concept for our model.

Detection of pose using IR camera

The baby may be in low lit conditions at times and we want to make sure that MediaPipe can detect images taken with an IR camera.

Demonstration of functionality

3.2.2 Data collection and labeling

With any machine learning project, access to a large, high-quality and accurately labeled dataset is essential. Therefore, making sure we had an adequate dataset was the first thing we did to determine the viability of this product. To find videos, our two most helpful resources were Youtube and cerebral palsy research papers.

We trained our prototype on a one hour video of labeled babies that pediatricians use when they are learning the General Movement Assessment[6]. This decision satisfied our requirement of using a dataset labeled by medical professionals.

We found two data sets from research papers that had anonymized data of just skeletal pose coordinates.

Categories of movement

There are two main movement indicators of cerebral palsy:

  1. Cramped synchronized movements: rigid, non-fluid movements where all limbs contract and relax almost simultaneously.
  2. Absent fidgety movements: the absence of small circular movements in the neck trunk and limbs.

3.2.3 Pose tracking

Motivation

A standard 720 x 576 pixel video has 414720 pixels with different RGB values. This is a lot of information to feed into an LSTM model. As a general rule, the more features you feed into a model, the more data you need for the model to determine which features are important to the classifying problem.

As we didn’t have much data to work with, we had to refine the feature space of our video. To do so, we instead fed the coordinates of each of the infant's limbs in each frame by building a skeletal model of the baby. This reduces the input features to 20 x, y and z coordinates.

Model comparison

There have been many pose estimation models developed in the past few years, with slight differences between all of them. As the majority of our dataset was lower resolution videos, different pose estimation models had different performance. We also needed a model that could do real-time detection on a CPU, as GPU hardware is more expensive and this model will need to run real-time in our monitor.

We compared three popular models: OpenPose, MediaPipe and Movenet. To verify the best model, we ran the models on 5 of our dataset videos and determined the difference by eye. While all three models performed well on higher resolution videos, MediaPipe performed much better on low resolution videos, as shown in Figure 3.

Therefore, we chose MediaPipe to:

  • Satisfy the constraint of detecting pose using low resolution videos
  • Satisfy the quality attribute of good pose tracking
  • Satisfy our functional requirement of live pose detection faster than 20 frames per second when running on a intel i7 CPU

Limitations and improving MediaPipe

MediaPipe still didn’t perform well on some videos taken from different angles. It gives a confidence score for each joint it identifies. When the confidence score was above 0.9, it identified the limbs properly. Thus, we could determine which videos were properly tracked and remove the bad ones from our dataset. Approximately 40% of our videos needed to be removed due to poor tracking.

Moving forward, we plan to improve MediaPipe on the low resolution videos by augmenting the model with videos specific to our dataset. MediaPipe offers model customization so that we can hand label the videos that were badly tracked, and then retrain MediaPipe on these videos. With this, we will be able to use the removed videos to train our model as well.

Figure 3 a) OpenPose limb tracking on test baby video. Infant’s right leg is badly tracked.b) MediaPipe limb tracking on the same video. All limbs are properly tracked. c) MediaPipe tracking another test video. It fails to label either of the legs of the left arm. This video had to be removed from the data set.

We also performed comparisons on different model complexities from MediaPipe to try and make tracking better. MediaPipe offered three options for model complexity, 0,1,2, normally defaulted to 1. We found that increasing the model complexity to 2 improves the tracking results in some cases, but also introduces more noise in the data, therefore we decided to stick with 1, the default condition to meet our functional requirement of good tracking.

3.2.4 Feature selection and Data Processing

Because of our limited dataset, our model is very vulnerable to overfitting to specific features of the data we are feeding in, and not to indicators of cerebral palsy itself. We wanted to minimize this risk as much as possible. We did this by manually feature selecting the relevant information from our data to input into the model. This allows our model to better train on a limited dataset, and for us to be able to use a less complicated model aiming for a more robust solution.

The first step in this process is educating ourselves on the General Movement Assessment, to learn what indicators we see, and estimating what a model might otherwise overfit to. This assessment showed us two key actions that infants do that are indicators of Cerebral Palsy, and the distinguishing factors of these actions:

  1. The absence of fidgety movements. This is shown in videos of infants through stiff limbs and no small circular movements.
  2. Cramped synchronized general movements. This is shown when two or more limbs cramp and move in the same general direction. The limbs move in unison, and overtime, you see the infant always cramping in the same general direction.

We wanted to see how these movements would show up in our data. So our first step was to look at our raw data, and understand the best way to interpret it.

MediaPipe gave us 33 landmark points on the infant's body that we can track the location of in time, shown in Figure 4. In order to get the lease “overfit” model, we wanted to feature select for only relevant information to be sent into the model.

Figure 4: Pose estimation from MediaPipe.

A key feature for CSGM is that the limbs are very “synchronized” in their movements, meaning that they move in unison. To test that this will show up on the data, I graphed the right and left feet, and the right and left wrist. While looking at these graphs we also made two more decisions in our data architecture:

  • We decided to crop our videos to 10s fragments, so that it is small enough for the LSTM to predict in a reasonable time frame, and limiting the input arrays to 250 units long (25 frames per second). But using 10s also gives enough time for a complete cramp to be shown in a single video.
  • We decided to augment our videos by overlapping the cropped portions, so for example in a 10s video, the first 5s is overlapped with the last 5s of the previous video. Therefore our model will be able to determine if a cramp happened in either the beginning or the end of the video, and we are less likely to overfit in what time the cramp might have occurred in that 10s timeframe.

3.2.5 Machine learning model

Model of choice: LSTM

We are tracking limb coordinates over time in order to classify a baby’s movements. Therefore we needed to use a model that can handle sequential data. We chose a Long Short Term Memory (LSTM) model due to the following reasons:

  • To implement the functional requirement of predicting using time-series data
  • To implement the functional requirement of using a model that has been tried and tested in the real world, since LSTMs have already been used for action recognition and speech recognition
  • To meet our time constraints of building a prototype in 3 months, since we found availability of Tensorflow libraries for instantiating an LSTM
  • To meet our knowledge constraints of having undergraduate level understanding of artificial intelligence,  since we found tutorials on the internet teaching to use LSTMs
  • To meet our functional requirement of making predictions live using camera data faster than 5 frames per second when running on a laptop with intel i7 CPU

Optimizer of choice: Adam

The constraint we want to minimize is the level of overfit the model that we train is to our data. We had a choice of a few different optimizers to choose from instantiated in Keras. While training, we noticed variability in training and validation accuracy from different training sessions of the exact same data. In some runs, we would reach 100% accuracy, and losses below 0.3, and in others, 40% accuracy and losses too large to calculate (e.g. 9999999999999). This would also happen in the very same training session, where the model could reach 100% accuracy in one epoch, and then 43% in the next. The model would get stuck in this global minimum and never be able to restore its previous accuracy because of the large loss values.  Because of this variability we decided to try different optimizers to ensure a more gradual gradient descent.

  • Adadelta:
  • Adadelta was chosen because of the two improvements
  • Uses a continual decay of learning rates throughout training.  
  • Don't need to set a global learning rate.
  • This model performed better in giving us more consistent training results, and never jumped to a lower accuracy score while training. However, sometimes during training runs it could get stuck in a local minimum, where the training accuracy would not go over a certain percentage. When this happened, we had to manually stop training, use Adam again to “kick” the model out of the local minimum, and then use Adadelta again.

Instead we found a work-around to use Adam throughout:

  • Adam, with a learning rate of 0.0001
  • This is usually defaulted to 0.001 for this optimizer, but with slowing the learning rate, we now were able to have more controlled training session where the variability of training accuracy between epochs reduced, and so it would reduce the likelihood of us getting stuck in a global minimum, but still able to “kick” us out of a local minimum.

Therefore Adam with a learning rate of 0.0001 was chosen.

Alternatives Model Types considered

One alternative we also considered was Transformers. Transformers are a new category of A.I that looks at sequential data as a whole object instead of as a sequence of small objects. Transformers are successful for natural language processing, such as seen with ChatGPT. They have been used for action recognition too[7], but we found that using Transformers would not fulfill our time constraints of 3 months to make a prototype due to the lesser availability of tutorials on the internet for them compared to LSTMs. We plan to use Transformers when the class is over, since our time constraints will be more flexible.

Live detection

We intend to detect abnormal movements live. Our live detection collects a 10 second segment of pose data, then applies our prediction model to this data to determine if the baby is doing cramped synchronized movements or fidgety movements. The model continually repeats this cycle of data collection and prediction.

When we run our live detection code on our laptops using our webcam to collect data, the speed we can loop the code is between 7 frames per second to 14 frames per second. To satisfy this constraint, we decided to train the models for detecting live movements with camera feed using data at a rate of 7 frames per second (e.g. the dance model)

4. Verification and Validation

4.1 Cerebral Palsy Model:

We performed leave-5-out validation and training for our model. This was chosen as the validation method to satisfy the constraint of the limitations in our dataset, and to overcome the problem of overlaps in both the validation and training set. We started with 26 videos of Normal movements (CP-)  (labeled 1-26) and 26 videos of cramped synchronized general movements (CP+)  (27 - 52).

To create the leave-5-out validation datasets, we first separated 5 sequential videos from the rest of the data, and labeled this as the “Test Set”. We wanted them to be in sequence because then none of the intermediate overlaps would be in the test set.  Since these videos were in order, they would all usually be in the same category - either all CP+ or CP-. So with the remainder of the data, we randomly chose 5 more videos, and this became the “Validation Set” to also be able to test the same model on if it only predicts one category, or is able to make both predictions. The rest of the data was used to train our model.  To summarize, we have:

  • Test Set: 5 sequential videos
  • Validation: 5 random videos
  • Training Set: 42 remaining videos.

We were able to detect gangnam style at 45 degrees to the camera, since we had video data with up to this angle, and it the model tended to fail at larger angles. We could also detect dance between 50cm and 3 meters from the camera

With the dance model we also learned how much data we needed for accurate detection of dance class using an LSTM. We learned that filming 6 different individuals, totalling 25 minutes of video data per category we classified, was enough to generalize to all people who tested the dance model on demo day.

Prototyping risks and mitigation steps

The dance classification model that was the most accurate was trained using 2 second videos. When we trained the model using 5 second video segments it was less accurate. This is a prototype risk because we were using segments of 10 seconds for infant movement classification, which is our intended use case.

4.3 Validation tests

4.3.1 Dance model in Demo Day

We invited visitors in demo day to test our dance model to validate how users perceived our prototype. We noticed that most people were amazed by the speed that we could detect which dance they were performing, as well as the accuracy of the detection. So we were able to validate that a LSTM model can achieve satisfactory speed and accuracy.

4.3.2 Strategies for validation of future requirements beyond MVP

The next validation will be to use our cerebral palsy detection in real infants. We will integrate our portable hardware (Jetson Nano) with our software to run our cerebral palsy detection on cribs of live with infants. Once we acquire more data, we want to validate that our true positive rate is above 90% for detecting fidgety movements and cramped synchronized movements in infants in the real world.

4.3.3 Prototyping risks and mitigation steps

One risk that we face is that we are using powerful hardware to run live movement detection (Intel i7 CPUs, or Jetson Nano). To meet our requirement of total hardware that costs less than $100, we may need to use slower processors and therefore reduce our ability to meet our requirement of live detection faster than 5 frames per second.

In case we can obtain a license for implementing the General Movement Assessment, we plan to file a dependent claim under patent US10565846B2 for video monitoring. January 2025 - Onwards

Our most protectable asset is the data we collect. Although we currently use open source

videos, we plan on keeping private the data we collect through our data plan addressed in the design section. This will give us more freedom to operate in a saturated market.

5.2 Related IP and Freedom to operate

Wikipedia documents the first baby monitor was launched in 1937, which used radio instead of wifi, and only audio instead of audio and video. Therefore the concept of a baby monitor is 86 years old. We found more than 25 different brands of baby monitor on Amazon. Therefore we see it’s a market that has little IP hindrances to enter, giving us more freedom to operate. Even though we found active patents for baby monitoring devices, we could not find information on patent wars between baby monitor brands, such as the ones seen in the smartphone industry. This finding gives us more assurance of our freedom to operate.

Below we will explore existing companies in the market that have similar products to our baby monitor, as well as active patents.

5.3 Active patents

We found an active patent filed in the US for detecting baby positions such as crying, awake and asleep, using a baby monitor and deep learning algorithm (https://patents.google.com/patent/US10565846B2/en?q=(baby+monitor+video)&oq=baby+monitor+video). Benjamin Lui, who filed the patent, launched a product called BabbyCam and is selling it online (https://babbycam.com/products/babbycam?variant=29121547654).

While our baby monitor’s key difference from other monitors on the market is detecting cerebral palsy, we also planned to detect sleep and crying. Therefore this patent could limit the range of features we can implement in our baby monitor. On the other hand, we can start selling our device in Canada, Europe and China since the patent was only filed in the US.
